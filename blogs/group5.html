(Automated) Fact-Checking in the Era of Deep Generative Models

Haorui He Xusen Xiao DATA 8018 Final Project - The University of Hong Kong April 2026

Trustworthy automated fact-checking now depends on more than raw accuracy. It depends on whether a system can retrieve relevant evidence, reason transparently about that evidence, generalize across heterogeneous sources, and remain robust when attackers learn to manipulate the same reasoning pipeline.

Overview

Three Shifts Define the Current Landscape

Shift 1: From patterns to evidence

Early fake-news detectors learned stylistic and behavioral patterns, but those cues generalized poorly across platforms and rarely produced convincing explanations.

Shift 2: From single agents to deliberation

Retrieval-augmented LLM systems brought explicit evidence into the loop, and debate-based verification showed how structured disagreement can improve adjudication.

Shift 3: From transparency to security

Once fact-checkers became more transparent and more agentic, attackers gained finer control over how to poison evidence and steer verdicts.

Read together, these shifts reveal a single theme: automated fact-checking becomes more useful when it moves closer to evidence and reasoning, but every gain in interpretability also changes the system's failure modes.

Problem Setting

Why Pattern-Based Fact-Checking Reached a Limit

Automated fact-checking emerged because the scale of misinformation outgrew what human fact-checkers could handle manually. In practice, the task is simple to state but difficult to do well: take a claim or news item as input, determine whether it is supported or refuted, and then provide an explanation strong enough to justify the verdict.

~86%

of global citizens are exposed to misinformation.

62%

of online content may be false in some environments, outpacing manual review.

High stakes

Economic losses, public harm, and trust erosion make reliable verification essential.

Input

Claims, news reports, posts, or multimodal content that needs verification.

Output

A verdict plus a justification that explains how the evidence supports that verdict.

Before large language models became central, most systems followed a pattern-recognition pipeline. Researchers extracted content features from text and images, or social-context features from user behavior and diffusion structure, and then trained classifiers to separate fake and real content. That approach was productive, but it remained fundamentally indirect: it inferred veracity from cues rather than checking the claim against external evidence.

BERT-EMO and the generalization gap

A strong pattern-based classifier can score well in a familiar environment and still fail once evaluation shifts to more diverse sources.

Pattern-based inference

Pattern-driven systems can label suspicious content quickly, but they often operate like black boxes. They correlate style, tone, or diffusion behavior with misinformation rather than grounding a decision in verifiable evidence.

Evidence-based verification

Human fact-checking is persuasive because it exposes the ruling, the reasoning, and the sources behind the conclusion. That shift from patterns to evidence is what makes generative AI useful in this space.

Methodological Shift

From Evidence Retrieval to LLM Agents

Retrieval-augmented fact-checking changes the task from pattern matching to evidence-centered reasoning. Instead of deciding from cues inside the content alone, the system first gathers potentially relevant evidence and then asks an LLM to adjudicate the claim in light of that evidence. This creates a more interpretable workflow, but it also exposes a new bottleneck: the final verifier still has to choose which evidence matters most.

Claim

Start from the statement or item that needs verification.

Retrieve

Collect web or knowledge-base evidence that may support or refute the claim.

Reason

Interpret, compare, and weigh conflicting evidence with an LLM-based verifier.

Justify

Return a verdict together with an explanation that readers can inspect.

Why single-agent verification can fail

Retrieved evidence is often noisy. When old, partial, or distracting evidence appears beside relevant evidence, a single verifier can still lock onto the wrong signal.

Example claim

"52% of Nigeria's current population lives in urban areas."

Outdated evidence 36% urban population in 2008
Current evidence 52% urban population in 2025

If the model overweights the outdated figure, retrieval was not the problem by itself. The adjudication step was.

Debate-based Verification

DebateCV and DebateSFT

One natural response to verifier brittleness is to stop treating claim verification as a one-shot decision. Professional fact-checking already relies on internal deliberation, so the same intuition can be transferred to LLM systems: let multiple agents argue from opposing positions, then let a moderator decide when the debate has converged and what verdict it supports.

Why debate is a useful analogy

Editorial verification is often strongest when competing interpretations are forced into the open before a ruling is finalized.

  • Weak arguments are easier to challenge.
  • Important evidence is less likely to be overlooked.
  • Final judgments become less dependent on a single viewpoint.
Structured disagreement becomes a verification tool.

What DebateCV changes

Cross-examination

Opposing debaters expose evidence conflicts that a single model might ignore.

Convergence control

The moderator decides whether another round is necessary or whether the debate is already sufficient.

Adjudication

The final verdict depends on the quality of moderation, not only on argument generation.

This also exposes the method's hardest problem. LLMs are often strong debaters but weaker judges, especially when debates grow longer or when the moderator falls into neutral defaults despite sufficient evidence.

Security Frontier

Agentic Fact-Checking Changes the Attack Surface

Accuracy and transparency do not exhaust the problem. As fact-checking systems become more agentic, they begin to decompose claims into sub-questions, retrieve evidence per sub-question, and aggregate the partial results into a final verdict. That architecture improves reasoning structure, but it also changes how attacks must be designed.

DEFAME

Dynamic tool use keeps retrieval and reasoning tightly coupled during verification.

InFact

Explicit decomposition organizes verification around multiple sub-questions and partial answers.

Claim decomposition

Breaking a claim into parts improves retrieval quality and exposes where evidence actually matters.

That structure explains why broad poisoning strategies weaken against agentic systems. If one malicious document only distorts one fragment of a larger verification chain, the final aggregation can still recover. The next generation of attacks therefore has to become more targeted and more justification-aware.

General poisoning

Craft broad evidence for the top-level claim and hope that retrieval repeats the same mistake often enough to sway the final verdict.

This becomes less reliable once verification is decomposed.

Why transparency becomes risky

A detailed justification tells readers what evidence mattered, but it also tells attackers where to intervene. Once sub-claims and evidence chains are visible, poisoning can become precise.

The blessing

Clear explanations help readers trust, inspect, and contest fact-checks.

The curse

The same explanations can become step-by-step guidance for targeted evidence poisoning.

Attack Analysis

Fact2Fiction and Justification-Aware Poisoning

Fact2Fiction responds directly to agentic fact-checking. Instead of poisoning retrieval with a generic false narrative, it decomposes the target claim, studies the victim's own justification, identifies which sub-claims and evidence items matter most, and then injects malicious evidence strategically where the verifier is most exposed.

Answer planning

Plan adversarial answers that directly contradict the key evidence highlighted by the verifier.

Budget planning

Allocate poisoning effort unevenly so the most influential sub-claims receive the strongest attack.

Query planning

Predict retrieval behavior so malicious evidence is more likely to appear exactly where it is needed.

Current defenses remain weak against this threat model. Paraphrasing can change the surface form of the claim, clustering-based detection can try to isolate malicious evidence in embedding space, and perplexity-based filters can look for unnatural phrasing. Yet none of these defenses fully neutralize targeted poisoning once the attacker is aligned with the verifier's own structure.

Paraphrasing

Test whether the attacker still succeeds when the system fact-checks a reworded version of the claim.

Malicious detection

Use clustering signals in embedding space to separate clean evidence from suspicious evidence.

PPL-based filtering

Flag documents whose phrasing looks unnatural or instruction-like under a language model.

Synthesis

Where the Field Stands Now

Automated fact-checking has progressed from pattern recognition to evidence-centered reasoning and then to agentic verification. Each step improved the system's ability to explain itself, but each step also exposed a new weakness. The current frontier is therefore not simply higher accuracy. It is the joint problem of evidence quality, reasoning quality, and attack resilience.

Stage Dominant strategy Main gain Main weakness
Pattern-based detection Learn cues from content and social context Efficient classification from existing signals Poor generalization and weak explainability
RAG-based verification Retrieve evidence, then reason over it Evidence-grounded verdicts and clearer explanations Brittle single-agent adjudication under noisy retrieval
Debate and agentic systems Use decomposition, role specialization, and deliberation Better structure, stronger justifications, improved robustness Higher system cost and new moderation challenges
Security-aware fact-checking Model targeted poisoning and defense explicitly More realistic view of deployment risk Defenses still lag behind attack sophistication

How can retrieval become more robust?

Better evidence ranking and filtering still matter before even the best verifier can succeed consistently.

How should moderator agents be trained?

Debate-based verification only works well if the moderator role becomes a first-class learning target.

Can transparency be preserved safely?

Readers still need clear justifications, but those justifications must not double as attack manuals.

What defenses work against targeted poisoning?

Current paraphrasing and detection heuristics are not enough for justification-aware attacks.

How should trustworthiness be measured?

Accuracy, evidence sufficiency, explanation quality, cost, and security need to be evaluated together.
